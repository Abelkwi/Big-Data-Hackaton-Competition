1. Project overview
Rwanda has made remarkable progress in agricultural development over the past two decades. Investments in agricultural productivity, improved inputs, climate-smart farming, and rural development. Nevertheless, improving the amount of food produced is not sufficient on its own: farmers also need effective post-harvest handling, storage and market access, while buyers and communities need reliable access to suitable and nutritious food. Fragmented information across production, prices, food availability, food security, and nutrition can make it harder for stakeholders to identify where further investigation or intervention may be needed.agricultural productivity, climate-smart farming, improved inputs, and rural development have significantly increased food production. 

However, **important structural challenges remain**: Many smallholder farmers continue to experience post-harvest losses, limited storage capacity, weak market access, and low bargaining power. At the same time, institutional buyers including schools, hotels, restaurants, hospitals, retailers, and food processors often struggle to secure reliable, high-quality supplies from local producers. These inefficiencies exist alongside persistent food insecurity, malnutrition, and youth unemployment.

National and international evidence demonstrates the urgency of addressing these challenges. Approximately 20.6% of Rwanda's population continues to experience food insecurity, 40-50% harvest in Rwanda is lost yearly due to structural inefficiencies (around 141kgs per person) that comes between the farmers(producers) and consumers which makes 12% of yearly GDP, While 20.6% of Rwandan population faces food insecurity of while 27% of children under five remain affected by stunting. Furthermore, studies indicate that significant quantities of food are lost or wasted across the value chain every year, representing not only economic losses but also wasted natural resources and missed opportunities to improve livelihoods and nutrition. (All statistics provided were examined at here: https://colab.research.google.com/drive/1ohqeb6bSejv5i7vXrSE7dwtvnMwpN4p6?usp=sharing ) 

SMARTSAVE RWANDA responds to these challenges by **introducing an integrated system that connects farmers, cooperatives, institutional buyers, logistics providers, youth entrepreneurs, and development partners within one collaborative ecosystem**. Rather than replacing existing agricultural programmes, SMARTSAVE RWANDA complements national efforts by improving coordination across the food system, increasing market efficiency, promoting responsible food utilisation, and strengthening local value chains.

The initiative is built around four strategic pillars:
•	Food Loss Reduction: Improving storage, aggregation, value addition, and responsible food recovery.
•	Market Connectivity: Linking farmers with reliable institutional and commercial markets through technology-enabled coordination.
•	Nutrition Improvement: Promoting equal access to nutritious food while reducing food waste.
•	Youth Empowerment: Creating employment, entrepreneurship, leadership, and innovation opportunities for young people within Rwanda's evolving food economy.

The initiative directly contributes to Rwanda's Vision 2050, the Second National Strategy for Transformation (NST2), and the Sustainable Development Goals, particularly SDG 1 (No Poverty), SDG 2 (Zero Hunger), SDG 4 (Quality Education) SDG 8 (Decent Work and Economic Growth), SDG 12 (Responsible Consumption and Production), SDG 13 (Climate Action) and SDG 17 (Partnership for the Goals).

Beyond improving agricultural markets, SMARTSAVE seeks to build a more connected, resilient, and inclusive food system where every harvest creates greater value, nutritious food reaches those who need it most, and young innovators become active contributors to Rwanda's sustainable development. SMARTSAVE RWANDA is therefore more than a digital system or an agricultural project. It is a scalable social enterprise that transforms fragmented food systems into connected opportunities; creating shared economic, social, and environmental value for farmers, institutions, communities, and future generations

*Actions:*
  1. File and worksheet audit: Record each source filename, worksheet, reference period, and unit of analysis.
  2. Variable review: Use source documentation, codebooks and data dictionaries to establish variable meanings and measurement units.
  3. Standardization: Normalize labels, dates, and formats only where the intended meaning is clear; preserve original values where appropriate.
  4. Missing-value checks: Distinguish unknown values, not-applicable values, nonresponse, and genuine zeros. Missing values are not automatically replaced with zero.
  5. Duplicate checks: Identify duplicates using the correct record identifiers and unit of analysis. Repeated crop, location, or date values are not automatically duplicates.
  6. Unit validation: Ensure that measurements such as tonnes, kilograms, hectares, prices, index values, and percentages are not mixed incorrectly.
  7. Outlier review: Investigate suspicious values before excluding or correcting them. Any exclusions must be documented.
  8. Dataset comparison and joins: Combine datasets only when their definitions, geographical levels, reference periods, units, and join keys are compatible.
  9. Survey considerations: Where NISR survey data are used to produce population estimates, consult the survey documentation for weights, strata, clusters and estimation guidance.
  10. Validation: Cross-check calculations against source definitions and appropriate published aggregates where feasible.

14. Analytical methodology and model
The project uses a data-first approach: the actual variables and their definitions determine which KPIs can be calculated. No KPI should be displayed unless its formula, input variables, units, denominator, and reference period are documented.
Potential indicators
The following indicators may be included only where the required variables are available and compatible:
Recorded crop production: sum of compatible production quantities for a clearly defined crop, place and period.
Crop yield: compatible crop production divided by an appropriate cultivated or harvested area measure. For example, tonnes per hectare require production in tonnes and area in hectares for the same crop and relevant period.
Productivity gap: `(benchmark yield − observed yield) / benchmark yield × 100`, only where a defensible, comparable benchmark exists.
Post-harvest loss rate: `measured quantity lost / compatible harvested quantity × 100`, only when the source contains compatible loss and harvest quantities.
Food price trend: a summary of prices for the same commodity, market/coverage, currency, unit, and price type across comparable dates.
CPI inflation: calculated or reported using the official index series and its documented basis; do not confuse an index level with a percentage change.
Food-security or nutrition indicators: summarized according to the source's documented population, denominator, survey design and reference period.
Analytical approach
The primary methods are descriptive analysis, grouped comparisons, appropriately defined indicator calculations and Excel visualizations. A ranking or prioritization score should only be added if the indicators and weighting rules are transparent and defensible. Any assumptions or weights must be documented, and sensitivity to reasonable alternative assumptions should be checked when feasible.
A predictive model is not claimed unless one has been implemented and evaluated using a defensible validation method. Similarly, descriptive associations between farming practices and yields do not establish causation. Scenario outputs, if included, must be labelled as hypothetical rather than achieved impacts.
Excel workflow
The analysis workbook is intended to organize the work into raw data, data dictionary, cleaned data, quality checks, derived indicators, summary tables and dashboard sheets. Dataset-specific tabs should be kept separate where the sources have different structures or levels of measurement.

18. Limitations and future improvements
Current limitations to verify and document
Data sources may differ in definitions, reference periods, geographical resolution, units and collection methods.
National food-security, nutrition, price-index, market-price and agricultural survey indicators are not interchangeable and should not be merged without a defensible method.
Public datasets may not contain real-time institutional-buyer demand, current farmer inventories, storage capacity, transport availability or transaction-level prices. Without those inputs, the prototype cannot claim to perform live buyer–seller matching or establish actual unmet demand.
Food-loss rates can only be calculated if the data provide compatible loss and harvest quantities. Estimates from different studies may not be directly comparable.
Survey-based population estimates may require weights and variance methods that cannot be replaced by simple unweighted averages.
Findings based on descriptive comparisons do not, by themselves, establish causal impacts.
Real-world effects on food loss, farmer income, food security, nutrition or youth employment require implementation and evaluation; they must not be presented as achieved by the prototype without evidence.
Potential future improvements
Add verified demand and procurement information from participating institutional buyers.
Integrate compatible storage, transport, weather, market-access or crop-loss measurements where reliable data are available.
Develop a validated, transparent prioritization model if the dataset supports it.
Conduct user testing with farmers, cooperatives and agricultural planners.
Evaluate the solution through a pilot using measurable outcome indicators.
Improve the dashboard's accessibility, filters and documentation.
Update the dataset registry and indicators as new official data become available.
